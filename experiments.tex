\subsection{Setup}
We generated MEKHs for germanium, gallium, cobalt, and boron with the configuration of Section~\ref{sec:runconfig}; Table~\ref{tab:transformations} gives their sizes. No expert annotation was available, so every measurement below is either computed from the pipeline's own artifacts or checked against public authoritative sources by the authors; all scripts, judge prompts, and curated files are in the supplementary material.

\subsection{RQ1: Are the hyperedges correct and grounded?}
\paragraph{Precision proxy.}
Two LLM judges, \JudgeAName{} and \JudgeBName{}, both from model families different from the generator (\GenModel{}), rated every hyperedge on three boolean criteria---is the process a real industrial or chemical transformation; are the listed precursors and products correct for it; does every HS code plausibly match its material---and an overall verdict, with a written rationale (prompt in supplementary). A hyperedge is \emph{fully correct} only if all three hold. Table~\ref{tab:judge} reports the fraction judged fully correct with bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals and Cohen's $\kappa$ between judges. Pooled over \JudgeNAll{} hyperedges, \JudgePrecisionAllClaude{} are fully correct according to \JudgeAName{} (\JudgePrecisionAllLlama{} according to \JudgeBName{}), ranging from \JudgePrecisionGalliumClaude{} for gallium to \JudgePrecisionCobaltClaude{} for cobalt. The three criteria serve as the error taxonomy, and they fail very unevenly. The processes themselves are almost always plausible (\JudgePlausibleGalliumClaude{}--\JudgePlausibleCobaltClaude{} per MEKH), and inputs and outputs are judged correct for \JudgeIOGalliumClaude{}--\JudgeIOGermaniumClaude{}; the HS codes, however, are judged correct for only \JudgeHSGalliumClaude{} (gallium), \JudgeHSBoronClaude{} (boron), \JudgeHSGermaniumClaude{} (germanium), and \JudgeHSCobaltClaude{} (cobalt) of hyperedges. Full correctness is therefore limited mainly by HS-code assignment, not by the discovery of processes. A typical case is the magnesiothermic reduction of boron trioxide to elemental boron: the judge accepts the process, its inputs, and its outputs, but rejects the codes because magnesium metal is coded as magnesium waste and scrap and elemental boron under an isotope heading. Because the MEKH is joined to trade data by code, this is the error that matters most for the application, and we return to it in Section~\ref{sec:lessons}. The judges are LLMs, so these figures are a proxy for precision, not a measurement of it.

\paragraph{Gate audit.}
Section~\ref{sec:gates} admits three kinds of hyperedge that an analyst should be able to see. Pooled over the four MEKHs, \MekhUnscoredAll{} of \MekhProcessesAll{} hyperedges were retained \emph{unscored} because some reference was still unreviewed after the last review round (\MekhUnscoredFracGermanium{} of germanium, \MekhUnscoredFracCobalt{} of cobalt, \MekhUnscoredFracBoron{} of boron, and \MekhUnscoredFracGallium{} of gallium hyperedges); \MekhUntypedEdgesAll{} carry at least one untyped (plain-name) material (per MEKH \MekhUntypedEdgesGermanium{}--\MekhUntypedEdgesBoron{}); and \MekhUnregisteredEdgesAll{} carry an HS code that is not registered as a material vertex (per MEKH \MekhUnregisteredEdgesCobalt{}--\MekhUnregisteredEdgesBoron{}). No retained hyperedge has a defined evidence score below $\tau$. All of these hyperedges are included in every measurement in this section.

\paragraph{Evidence grounding.}
The admission gates require references, but a reference can be cited without supporting the claim. For every hyperedge we fetched the cached content of each cited reference and tested, with alias-aware string matching, whether it mentions at least one input and at least one output. Table~\ref{tab:evidence} shows that \EvidenceFullGermanium{} (germanium) to \EvidenceFullBoron{} (boron) of hyperedges are fully grounded in this sense and \EvidenceAnyGermanium{}--\EvidenceAnyCobalt{} have at least one input or output mentioned; \EvidenceRefsFetchedCobalt{}--\EvidenceRefsFetchedBoron{} of cited references had retrievable content at evaluation time. This is a lower bound on grounding (a page may describe a process without naming a material as the MEKH names it), and an upper bound on what an analyst can verify by following the link.

\paragraph{Recall of canonical routes.}
We curated small sets of canonical routes---\RoutesNBoron{} for boron, \RoutesNGallium{} each for gallium and germanium, \RoutesNCobalt{} for cobalt---from the USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries~\cite{usgsmcs2025} and standard chemical references, each expressed as HS-6 input and output sets with its source (supplementary). A route is recovered \emph{leniently} if some hyperedge shares an input and an output code with it and \emph{strictly} if the hyperedge's code sets contain the route's. Table~\ref{tab:recall} shows lenient/strict recall of \RecallLenientBoron{}/\RecallStrictBoron{} for boron but at most \RecallLenientGermanium{} lenient for the other three. With four to eight routes per mineral, each route moves recall by 12--25 points, and a lenient match can arise from coincidental code overlap: the cobalt ore-to-hydroxide route matches leniently although the MEKH contains only the leaching of scrap, not of primary ore. Several misses are coding problems rather than missing chemistry: gallium arsenide is coded inconsistently (as a gallium-metal article and under semiconductor-device and doped-element codes) but never under its own heading, trimethylgallium carries a non-existent HS code, and germanium tetrachloride is coded inconsistently across processes, so routes the MEKH describes do not match by code; and no hyperedge electrowins cobalt from cobalt sulfate, the route's input: electrowinning appears only within scrap-recycling processes. Other routes are absent: there is no MOCVD route from trimethylgallium to gallium nitride, nor the lithium-cobalt-oxide cathode chemistry, and zone refining of germanium metal. For boron, the missed routes are the dehydration of boric acid to boron oxide and the synthesis of hexagonal boron nitride; all misses are listed in the supplementary material.

\subsection{RQ2: What does bottom-up discovery add over the top-down view?}
The USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries~\cite{usgsmcs2025} are the reference top-down view an analyst starts from; the boron chapter lists \CoverageUsgsBoron{} HS-6 codes. We compare them with the HS-6 codes of the MEKH's registered material vertices; this is conservative, since hyperedges reference further, unregistered codes. The boron MEKH registers \CoverageMekhBoron{} codes, including all of the USGS codes, and \CoverageMekhOnlyBoron{} of them (\CoverageFracBoron{}) do not appear in the USGS listing (Table~\ref{tab:coverage}). The MEKH-only codes used by the most processes are of two kinds: boron-fluorine intermediates---boron trifluoride and potassium fluoroborate, each with a production route in the MEKH that \JudgeAName{} rated fully correct---and bulk reagents consumed by boron processes, such as sulfuric acid, soda ash, and ammonia. The count overstates the mineral-specific difference in two ways: some MEKH-only codes are generic utilities (hydrogen, oxygen, water), and some reflect code errors (RQ1 finds only \JudgeHSGalliumClaude{}--\JudgeHSCobaltClaude{} of hyperedges with all codes correct; e.g., the code used for water, 285100, is not a valid current HS-6 code). The MEKH-only fraction is similarly high for germanium (\CoverageFracGermanium{}), gallium (\CoverageFracGallium{}), and cobalt (\CoverageFracCobalt{}), although their most-used MEKH-only codes are mostly utilities. This is the quantitative form of the hidden-intermediate argument of Baldwin et al.~\cite{baldwin2023hidden}: many of the codes a mineral's transformation network depends on are not the ones the commodity view enumerates.

\subsection{RQ3: Are derived rankings stable across generations?}
Generation is stochastic, so a MEKH is one sample from a distribution over hypergraphs. We generated \RboRuns{} independent boron MEKHs with the same configuration, one of them the boron MEKH evaluated above, and computed the criticality list (Section~\ref{sec:application}) of each with $m_{base}=$ \RboBase{}. Figure~\ref{fig:degree} shows the in- and out-degree distributions (number of hyperedges producing or consuming a material) across the \RboRuns{} generations: most materials take part in one or two processes, a few in many, and the shape is similar across generations. We aggregate the \RboRuns{} lists by classic Borda count: in each list a material earns one point for every material ranked strictly below it (zero if absent), and points are summed over lists. We compare lists by extrapolated rank-biased overlap (RBO)~\cite{webber10rbo} at persistence $p=\RboPersistence{}$, which puts about half of the weight on the top \RboHalfWeightDepth{} positions~\cite{webber10rbo}; ties are broken at random and results averaged over \RboTieSamples{} tie-breaks. Against the Borda aggregate, RBO is \RboMean{}~$\pm$~\RboSd{} (mean~$\pm$ sample s.d.; range \RboMin{}--\RboMax{}), and on average \RboTopTenOverlap{} of a run's top \RboTopK{} are in the aggregate's top \RboTopK{}. Because the aggregate contains each run, the run-vs-run comparison is the stricter one: over all \RboPairs{} pairs of generations, RBO is \RboPairwiseMean{}~$\pm$~\RboPairwiseSd{} and two runs share \RboPairwiseTopTenOverlap{} of their top \RboTopK{} materials. Agreement is therefore moderate: roughly half of a generation's top \RboTopK{} agrees with the consensus, the lists are not interchangeable, and an analyst should read criticality from the aggregate of several generations rather than from one.
