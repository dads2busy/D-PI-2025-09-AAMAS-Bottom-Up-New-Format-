\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Advanced technologies depend on long industrial transformation chains from ores to intermediate chemicals and components. Concentrated in one supplier or country, these chains become failure points at multiple stages: environmental restrictions on rare-earth mining have collapsed heavy-rare-earth output~\cite{zhang2024reshaping}, and technological risk in the lithium supply network propagates disproportionately through a small set of midstream nodes~\cite{hu2025technological}. Analysts work \emph{top-down} from product to supplier records; Baldwin et al.~\cite{baldwin2023hidden} show this underestimates exposure, since inputs to inputs hide behind opaque networks~\cite{colon2022systemic}.

\paragraph{Application context.}
This work was carried out with, and is used by, supply-chain analysts at a university national-security policy research institute working under contract to a U.S. government intelligence-community sponsor. Their recurring question is concrete: for a given critical mineral, \emph{which intermediate materials and processes lie between the mine and the technology, which of them are concentrated in a few exporters, and what fails downstream if one is cut off?} Answering it requires a map of material transformations that is (i) typed by the Harmonized System (HS) codes in which trade is actually recorded, so that it can be joined to trade data, and (ii) traceable to documentary evidence, so that an analyst can audit any link before acting on it. No such map exists for most critical minerals, and building one by hand does not scale. The same HS-typed classification that the map is built on also feeds the analysts' other tools: it supplies the commodity-to-code buckets that join trade-flow data to their dependency-network explorer (Section~\ref{sec:application}).

\paragraph{Approach.}
We built MP-DNF, an agentic pipeline that constructs such a map \emph{bottom-up}. Starting from a seed mineral, six role-specialized LLM agents (classification, material finding, process discovery, process merging, reference review, and source scoring) propose materials and industrial processes, retrieve supporting documents through Model Context Protocol (MCP) tools, and score the evidence. The output is a \emph{Material Evolution Knowledge Hypergraph} (MEKH): a directed labeled multi-hypergraph whose vertices are HS-6-coded materials and whose hyperedges are processes mapping input sets to output sets (Section~\ref{sec:mekh}). The agents propose; deterministic gates dispose. A process enters the MEKH only if its evidence score (defined only once every cited reference has been reviewed and at least two of them, spanning distinct domains, score well) clears a threshold, and its LLM-assigned operating-scale score clears a floor (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}); materials are typed with HS-6 codes that the classification, finder, and manufacturing agents assign with a code-search tool, though codes are not independently verified against the official nomenclature. Every hyperedge therefore carries the evidence an analyst needs to audit it.

\paragraph{Research questions.}
An earlier presentation of this system reported network statistics on the constructed hypergraphs but did not establish whether the hypergraphs were correct or whether bottom-up discovery adds anything over a top-down view. We address that directly:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (accuracy and grounding).} Are the discovered transformations factually correct (process, inputs and outputs, HS codes) and are they actually supported by the references cited?
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (coverage beyond top-down).} Does bottom-up discovery surface intermediate materials that the official top-down commodity view omits?
  \item \textbf{RQ3 (robustness).} Are the criticality rankings derived from a MEKH stable under the stochasticity of LLM-driven generation?
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1)~A deployed agentic pipeline, MP-DNF, in which LLM agents with MCP tools construct evidence-grounded, HS-typed knowledge hypergraphs of material transformations, with explicit symbolic admission gates (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}).
(2)~An evaluation that requires no expert annotation and answers RQ1--RQ3 for MEKHs of boron, gallium, germanium, and cobalt: a two-judge LLM precision proxy with bootstrap confidence intervals and inter-judge agreement, an evidence-support audit of cited references, recall against curated canonical routes, and a comparison with the USGS commodity listing (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
(3)~A criticality measure on hypergraph reachability and a sensitivity method based on rank aggregation, and a disruption analysis coupling the boron MEKH to UN Comtrade trade data that identifies refined borax as the concentrated intermediate whose loss cascades furthest downstream (Section~\ref{sec:application}).
(4)~Lessons from deployment with analysts, including failure modes of LLM-generated supply-chain knowledge and the limits of LLM-judge validation (Section~\ref{sec:lessons}).
